Cancer remains a major public health concern, with the number of cancer cases worldwide estimated to reach **28.4 million by 2040**. [Lifestyle factors](https://biomedfacts.com/%CF%85%CE%B3%CE%B5%CE%AF%CE%B1/2026/09/12/karkinos-5-tropoi-gia-na-meiwseis-ton-kindyno-anapty3hs.html), including smoking, physical activity, diet, alcohol consumption and body mass index (BMI), have been identified as important modifiable risk factors for cancer.

## **But could 5 everyday habits be linked to cancer risk?**

A large new study published in the ***[British Journal of Cancer](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41416-026-03613-9)*** analysed data from **287,745 people** and found that participants who met 4 or 5 criteria for a healthier lifestyle had a lower risk of **13 of the 27 cancer types** examined, compared with those who met only 0 or 1 criterion.

However, this was a **prospective observational cohort study**, not a randomised clinical trial. This means the findings show associations; they do not prove that following all five habits will necessarily prevent cancer.

## **What did the researchers investigate?**

The researchers used data from the **UK Biobank** and followed **287,745 adults** for a median of 11.5 years.

Each participant received a lifestyle score ranging from 0 to 5, depending on how many of five healthy lifestyle criteria they met.

## **For each participant, the researchers assessed:**

1. **Smoking:** participants had either never smoked regularly or, if they were former smokers, had a smoking history of less than 30 pack-years — for example, less than one pack of cigarettes per day for 30 years.
2. **Alcohol consumption** within the limits defined by the study: up to 12 g of alcohol per day for women and up to 24 g per day for men.
3. **Diet:** participants with the highest scores on the study’s diet quality index — specifically, those in the top 40% — received one point toward their overall healthy lifestyle score.
4. **Adequate physical activity**
5. **BMI** between 18.5 and 25 kg/m²

## **What did the results show?**

During the follow-up period, **12,947 participants were diagnosed with cancer**.

People with a high healthy lifestyle score — meaning they met 4 or 5 of the 5 criteria defined by the researchers — had an overall **hazard ratio (HR) of 0.71** for developing cancer compared with those who met only 0 or 1 criterion.

This corresponds to an approximately **29% lower relative risk** of developing cancer overall in the group with the healthiest lifestyle score. However, this does not mean that every individual would experience a 29% reduction in their personal cancer risk.

## **Which cancers were associated with a lower risk?**

A higher healthy lifestyle score was associated with a lower risk of 13 types of cancer:

- **Oral cancer**
- **Pharyngeal and laryngeal cancers**
- **Oesophageal and stomach cancers**
- **Colorectal cancer**
- **Liver and pancreatic cancers**
- **Lung cancer**
- **Kidney cancer**
- **Bladder cancer**
- **Soft-tissue sarcoma**
- **Postmenopausal breast cancer**

## **Which factor appeared to matter most?**

Among the five lifestyle factors examined, **smoking stood out the most**.

Not smoking was associated with a lower risk of **12 of the 27 cancer types** studied, with particularly strong associations observed for lung, laryngeal, and pharyngeal cancers.

The authors concluded that, within the context of this study, **smoking and excess body weight were the lifestyle factors most frequently associated with the cancer types investigated.**

## **What does the study not prove?**

The study does not prove that these five behaviours directly caused the observed reduction in cancer risk.

This was a **prospective observational cohort study**, not a randomised clinical trial. This means that other differences between participants may also have influenced the results.

## **Limitations reported by the researchers**

- **The participants were not fully representative of the general population.** This means we cannot assume that the findings apply in exactly the same way to everyone.
- **Most participants were White.** As a result, it remains uncertain whether the same findings would apply equally to other population groups.
- **People who volunteer for studies like this are often healthier than the general population.** They may pay more attention to their health or undergo health checks more frequently.
- **Much of the information was self-reported by participants.** Some people may therefore have misremembered or underestimated behaviours such as how much they drank, smoked or exercised.
- **Other factors may also have influenced the results.** Even with statistical adjustments, it is impossible to perfectly account for every difference between the groups.
- **Participants’ habits may have changed over time.** Someone may have stopped smoking, gained weight or started exercising after their lifestyle was initially recorded.
- **There were relatively few cases of some rare cancers.** This means the findings for these cancer types are less certain.
